A reading guide to one module

The relay wire seam, in plain language

One module that owns the wire: connect to relays, target them, query them, subscribe to them, publish to them and count over them — and own the semantics those run on, with no store, no cache and no relay policy of its own. This page explains the job, the contract, the bounds, the refusals, the measurements, a block that was found and cleared — and the parts that are not finished.

CHAPTER 01

What the module is, and why it exists

One module that owns the wire, and only the wire. Everything below is in the spec's own words somewhere; this chapter is the plain-language reading of them.

The job, in the spec's own sentence

“Own the wire: connect to relays, target them, query them, subscribe to them and publish to them, and own the semantics those four run on (EOSE, retries, reconnection, connection health), with no store, no cache and no relay policy of its own.” One amendment, recorded in the spec's body, sharpens one of those four words: the module owns the policy — where the relay set, the read's ceiling and the caller's deadline are declared and bounded — while the mechanism (how many retries, how long it waits) is the transport's own defaults, read in the installed package and stated as such. Nothing in this spec claims one of the vendor's retry numbers as a guarantee.

Where it came from, said exactly

The module is the destination of the market's own relay code — the io.ts family at the pinned commit (104 lines plus 196 in the AppleSauce variant, listed in the spec's front matter). It is not an extraction: the spec was written first, at the module's intended final state, and the TypeScript implementation came after it as the first of that contract's implementations.

Why a seam, and why the vendor is not the contract

The pool request shape is adopted rather than invented — the same (relays, filters) => events contract that the vendor documents as satisfied by its own pool, by nostr-tools' SimplePool, by NDK and by nostrify's NPool. That is what makes the seam library-free as well as store-free: what is genuinely ours is the specified contract, the store-free guarantee, the reason vocabulary, the injected relay policy and the multi-backend proof — the vendor supplies sockets, not the contract. Two named second implementations (a nostrify NPool adapter and a Go relay host) are named as satisfiable, and neither exists yet — chapter 8 carries that honestly.

What it refuses to be, in one list

Not a store, and the store-free guarantee is structural, not a grep: a hand-written pool records every call and throws from every method, and the case requires the seam's construction to make zero pool calls. Not a relay owner: the relay set arrives as the host's answer on every call, and the module may not add, prefer, reorder or blacklist a relay. Not a cache and not a local write: publish reports one ack per targeted relay and stores nothing. Not a completeness oracle: query reports the EOSE it reached and stops there. Not a filter validator — that is a different module by name. And not a global: two seams in one process share no socket, no subscription and no relay set.

CHAPTER 02

The contract — six operations, four exports, nineteen types

What a consumer may call, what a host must supply, and the one mechanical diff that keeps the spec's lists and the code's exports from drifting apart.

01 connect

Reports the health of the relay set as the pool observes it, read under the key the pool files a relay by rather than the spelling the policy used. It dials nothing and waits for nothing: the vendor pools lazily, so a fresh seam truthfully reports every relay as not connected. A URL that is not a relay at all is a typed refusal, never an invented “not connected”.

02 query

Resolves with what arrived and whether the wire's boundary was reached — the EOSE fact, and nothing more. It never invents completeness: telling a cold answer from a complete one is the composition's fact, so the wire reports EOSE and stops there.

03 subscribe

Owns the subscription's lifetime. Cancellation is idempotent — unsubscribe() twice closes once and throws nothing — and a relay failure inside a live subscription is reported on the error channel rather than ending the caller's stream silently.

04 publish

Reports one acknowledgement per targeted relay, each carrying that relay's own answer, and performs no local write. An event no relay accepted is not stored by this module: what gets stored is the composition's policy, not the seam's.

05 count

The wire's own NIP-45 count — a different operation from the store's local count, and the spec names that asymmetry instead of papering over it. Over a target set it is the sum of the relays' own indices, reported approximate whenever any relay answered approximately.

06 close

Releases what the seam holds. Safe to repeat; nothing is left half-attached.

The nineteen types, in one line each

Raw nostr-tools events are the event view; the adopted pool shapes are NostrRequest and UpstreamPool; the read answers with a WireResult (events, the EOSE fact, and a failures channel); the live streams answer with Subscription and Unsub; the writes answer with RelayAck and PublishOutcome; the count answers with CountResult; and the rest are the module's nouns — RelayUrl, RelaySet, RelayReason, RelayStatus, FilterIn, Event, QueryOptions, SubscribeOptions, RelayPolicy, NostrIoOptions and NostrIo. The spec's list and the entry's export block are diffed name by name; today they are equal.

One library type survives by name, on purpose

The first implementation's stream type is rxjs' Observable — the vendor contract's own shape — and the spec names that as a declared exception, not an accident: a second implementation types its stream as its own equivalent and adapts at its boundary. The compiler configuration is strict (tsc --noEmit), so the contract compiles, and the typecheck is not a detector of a permitted library type.

CHAPTER 03

The bounds the module owns: the ceiling and the clock

A seam that can hang its caller is not a seam. Two bounds are declared here as inspectable values, and two behaviours are stated exactly rather than left to be discovered.

The ceiling is one constant, and the spec says why it exists

An unbounded array is the failure mode this bound answers: a relay that streams a large history cannot grow one read without bound. The value lives at the module's entry as const MAX_EVENTS_PER_READ = 10_000 — the evidence capture reads that exact line — so it is inspectable rather than buried in a branch. The case that holds it feeds one event past the boundary and requires the read to stop at exactly 10 000.

The deadline is a wall clock, and the spec had to correct itself twice to say so

A first reading treated the budget as an inactivity gap — a wire that goes quiet trips it, a wire that drips forever never does. A red-team probe measured the dripping case and showed the read stayed pending; the spec's reading now says a wall-clock budget on the whole read, and the case drives a dripping wire against it. The ceiling statement's original also belongs here: a read stopped by the bound reports the boundary as not reached, exactly as a read stopped by its deadline does.

What a read waits for when nothing bounds it

The read completes when every targeted relay reaches the boundary. A caller who passes no deadlineMs and whose relay never reaches EOSE simply waits, and what bounds that in practice is the transport's own per-relay timeouts — the vendor's ten-second event timeout and twenty-second EOSE ping timeout, read in the installed package and never executed, never a bound this module invents. The spec says that in those words; this page keeps the distinction.

What a count over a set is

Not a distinct-event count. The vendor's record is keyed by each relay's own URL and emits only once every targeted relay has answered, so a wire count is a fact about relays: two relays holding the same event are two indices, and neither the seam nor the vendor merges them. A relay that refuses or times out its own COUNT refuses the whole operation rather than being quietly dropped from the sum.

CHAPTER 04

Six refusals, and never a bare error

The rule: a refusal is a value from a stated six-name vocabulary, delivered on whatever channel the operation already has — never a bare string, never a silent absence, never a library error. Click any name to see what produces it and where it lands.

Where a refusal lands, and what does not reject

The operations that return a promise carry refusals in the answer they already return: WireResult.failures is a read's failure channel, beside events and eose, and each entry is the module's own typed refusal with a reason from the vocabulary. subscribe carries them on errors. A rejection through the module's own mapping is for the caller's own errors — a malformed filter, a relay policy that cannot answer, a relay set the transport cannot open. A relay's own refusal does not reject a read, and a policy the host cannot answer with never reaches the wire.

The absolute that was false, and is now the rule

An early reading of the module stated, as fact, that a bare Error from an injected pool never reaches the caller. Two independent red teams measured the opposite on the live subscription channels: a pool whose own events, eose or failures stream died delivered the raw library error to the caller, ended a live stream mid-flight, and left the channel typed as the module's own refusal empty. The repair put a mapping on every channel and inside every operation's guard, so what the module types as its own refusal always is one. Chapter 5's mutations 11, 12 and 13 are the proof that those paths can still fail; chapter 6 tells the story of how the hole was found.

Why the mapping reads the relay's own words

A relay is allowed to say why it refused, and NIP-01's machine-readable prefixes carry that reason. The vendor's own layer turns such a frame into a thrown error, and its group layer hands that throw over as a plain error message. An early implementation answered one generic rejection for every error and discarded the relay's own text — which made auth-required and rate-limited unreachable on the real path, while the test that asserted them passed against a hand-made message the vendor never sends. The repair reads the error's own reason. That is the one place the earlier text was over-broad rather than merely silent, and the spec records it as such.

CHAPTER 05

The rules, and how each was attacked on purpose

A test that cannot fail proves nothing. Twenty-one times, one rule was broken in the real file, the case named for it had to go red, the file was put back byte-for-byte and the case had to go green again. Every restore was checked by a fingerprint.

The store-free guarantee — proved by construction, not by a grep

The rule. The seam works with zero endpoints that are not relays: no store is injected and none is kept.

The attack. The seam was made to reach the pool while it was still being constructed, so a recording pool that throws from every method was reached at build time. The named case failed — the pool was called with status before any verb was asked for — then the file was restored and the case went green. Mutation 1. The companion case requires the seam's call count to be zero when it is asked for nothing.

The import allow-list — independence as a tree fact

The rule. Every import specifier in the implementation is module-relative or one of the module's own declared dependencies; no store, database or cache package may appear.

The attack. An import 'applesauce-core' line — the store package — was added to the entry. The allow-list command went red and was restored. Mutation 8. The module's own evidence asserts the positive form too: eleven specifiers, sorted and unique, and no applesauce-core — even though that package is present in node_modules as a transitive dependency of the transport.

Relay policy is injected, never owned — and de-duplication is not a preference

The rule. The relay set arrives as the host's answer on every call, and the module may not add, prefer, reorder or blacklist a relay.

The attack. The seam was made to hand the pool the host's array as-is again, so a relay the policy named twice was targeted twice. The case asserting the pool received the relay once failed; restored, green. Mutation 16. The spec's reading is precise about the one narrowing: the set handed to the pool is the unique one, in the host's own order, and two spellings of one relay are not merged — a repeated URL is the same relay asked twice, so de-duplication is what keeps a read from waiting for an answer that is not coming.

A refusal mid-read is a value, not a rejection

The rule. A relay refusing mid-read resolves the read with the events that arrived and the boundary as not reached, and carries the refusal as a value.

The attack. The refusal was dropped instead of carried, so the read invented a reached boundary and the failures list was silently empty. The case failed, and the same mutation was run against both conformance backends to require one outcome object from each. Mutation 10. A second attack fired the deadline but made the read report the boundary as reached — a cold answer scored as a complete one. Mutation 3.

The vocabulary is total, and the live channels are typed

The rule. A refusal is a value from the six-name vocabulary, never a bare string and never a silent absence; a failure on a live subscription is reported on the error channel rather than ending the caller's stream.

The attacks, four of them. The reason mapping was made to answer the generic rejection for a rate-limited refusal (mutation 7); the vendor path's own prefix was collapsed back to the generic rejection (mutation 9); the pool's events channel was passed through unmapped so the library's bare error reached the caller and ended a live read (mutation 11); and the pool's failures channel was left unmapped so the channel the module types as its own refusal ended on the library's error (mutation 12). A fifth attack rethrew a policy's own error unmapped — the pre-repair shape, where the policy read sat outside its operation's guard (mutation 13). Each named case went red and each file was restored.

One acknowledgement per targeted relay

The rule. A publish reports one ack per targeted relay, each carrying that relay's own answer, and a silent relay is reported rather than dropped.

The attacks. The acks were made to stop being lined up against the target set, so a relay that answered was treated as one that did not (mutation 5); and later, an ack map was keyed by the host's raw spelling while the pool files every answer under its own normalised key — so a relay that accepted was reported as refusing the event (mutation 20). Both restored; both green.

The count: it settles, it carries the flag, it is a sum

The rules. A wire count reports whether the relay's own index was approximate; over a target set it is the sum of the relays' own indices; and the operation returns an answer at all.

The attacks. The adapter was made to stop reading NIP-45's approximation flag, fabricating an exact count (mutation 6); and the awaited operator was restored to one that resolves only on completion while the vendor's COUNT stream never completes — so the count never settled and the case's own guard caught it instead of hanging the runner (mutation 19). That last one is chapter 6's finding B1. A target set naming one relay in two spellings was made to never settle, by counting the host's raw spellings at the boundary gate (mutation 21, finding B2's read half).

The bounds, and the boundary at close

The rules. A read stops at the stated ceiling, a dripping wire is bounded by the wall-clock deadline, relay health is read under the pool's own key with a non-relay URL refused, and closeOnEose ends a stream at the boundary, after the events that preceded it. Each was attacked: the ceiling guard removed (mutation 17, one event past the bound); the deadline disarmed (mutation 18); health looked up under the policy's raw spelling (mutation 14); and the pre-repair gate restored, so an EOSE the pool already held completed the output before the events (mutation 15). All restored, all green.

And the fingerprints are still right today

The falsification run prints each file's sha256 before the mutation and re-checks it after the restore; the capture records every one as restore verified: yes and closes with the tree “as it was before this run”. So “restored byte-for-byte” is not a line in a log — it is the file on disk being the file the measurement was taken from.

CHAPTER 06

A block, found and cleared

The most interesting thing this module has is not a green test. It is that its first version did not freeze: independent reviewers blocked it, twice, and both blocks were reproduced with executed evidence before they were repaired. This chapter is that story, told in order.

What the first block was: an absolute that was not true

The frozen entry claimed, unconditionally, that a bare Error from an injected pool never reaches the caller. One red team drove a pool whose events stream emitted one event and then errored: the caller's event stream ended mid-flight on the raw library error, and the channel typed as the module's own refusal delivered nothing. A second red team independently selected the same invariant as the module's highest-risk claim and reproduced it, concluding the hardening was bounded rather than architectural. The same first block found a second defect: a refusal inside query discarded every event that had already arrived, and the two conformance backends returned different outcomes for one scripted wire — a spec-versus-implementation contradiction, so the repair waited for the adjudicator to rule rather than the builder choosing a side.

What the second block was: two defects the whole evidence tree could not see

After the first repair, a blind pass ran its own out-of-tree probes against the real vendor package and blocked again on two findings:

  • B1 — the count could never settle. The adapter awaited a completion the vendor's COUNT stream never reaches: the vendor roots that stream in a replaying subject that never completes, so an operator that resolves only on completion waits forever, and count — unlike query — takes no options and so has no deadline to escape with. The first emission already holds the whole record, so the correct operator resolves on it. The whole evidence tree was blind to this because every count case ran against a stub whose stream does complete — a fixture that differed from production in exactly the dimension the operator depends on.
  • B2 — a relay's own identity was compared against the host's spelling. The pool files every relay answer under its own normalised key, while the seam matched acks against the host's raw spelling. A relay that answered “accepted” was therefore reported as refusing the event; and a target set naming one relay in two spellings could never reach its boundary and never settled. The two conformance backends could not see it because both echoed the host's spelling back — the harness's model of the vendor was asymmetric exactly where the new code depended on it.

The repair reused the one identity predicate the module already owned in the two places that lacked it, and taught the fixture to model the vendor's own normalisation — the same behaviour the earlier repair had relied on for connect.

What the module's reviewers caught in the module's own paperwork

The freeze record is unusually honest about its own side: it records that a builder's hand-off claimed a criterion was met — the multi-backend case retitled — and that it was not; the reviewer read the file itself rather than taking the report's word, and the freeze names the cost (one line) and the owner (the next increment that opens the module). It also records a second open item: the transport-port type a host is meant to implement is not re-exported by the contract entry, which is fine while nothing consumes it and a spec question the moment a host implements the port by name.

CHAPTER 08

What is not yet true

This chapter is deliberately uncomfortable. Everything below is a limit of the module as it stands, taken from its own records of what it did not verify — not a caveat, and not softened.

1 · No consumer inside this assembly imports it

The composition that will import the seam — event-access — is being built right now, and until it lands, the module's own freeze record says it plainly: what the module does not yet have is a consumer inside this assembly. Its consumers are named in its front matter and are real intentions, not a current fact.

2 · No socket is opened by any case, and the relays are hand-written fakes

Every case runs against the module's own test support: a fake pool that records calls and throws, a recording-and-answering hand-written pool, and a vendor stub that models the shipped transport's behaviour. The evidence reads 0 socket constructors, fetches or servers anywhere in the module's sources and tests. So everything that only shows up over a real connection is untested here: a slow relay, a dropped socket, a reconnection, a NIP-42 handshake, the vendor's keep-alive. The freeze names the “live-relay last mile” as an open ledger item, READ in the installed bytes and never executed, by design.

3 · The two named second backends do not exist

The contract's whole claim to being a spec-with-many-implementations is that a nostrify NPool adapter and a Go relay host can satisfy the same contract. Neither is in this assembly, and the second-backend case that does run drives the module's own hand-written pool — structural evidence for the contract's adaptiveness, not proof against those two backends.

4 · The vendor survey's claims are read, not executed

The spec's own findings section states it: the assembled document was not measured against the vendor's bytes, and the cited claims carry an UNVERIFIED label. The one exception is named: the four citations the two repairs depend on were re-read in the installed copies — the relay layer's NIP-01-prefix table and its throw, the group layer's conversion of that throw, the retry and timeout defaults, and the pool's normalising URL helper. Those are READ; the rest keep the label.

5 · Two open items ride with the freeze

  • N1 — a criterion the freeze records as unmet. A case was to be retitled as part of a repair; the builder's hand-off said it was done; the freeze's author checked the file and it was not. Recorded with a one-line cost and an owner rather than fixed by the record's author, who is read-only on source.
  • N2 — the transport-port type is not re-exported. The entry a host injects through (NostrIoOptions.pool) names a port type that the contract's export block does not carry. By the repair's own design, and an open question for the seam's next increment: if a host is ever meant to implement that port by name, the name has to enter the contract's type list, and that is a spec change with a criterion.

The honest sentence for all of chapter 8

The contract, the bounds, the reason vocabulary, the store-free guarantee and the injected policy are written down and executed — 24 green cases and 21 falsifications short of nothing. What is missing is not effort: it is a consumer, a socket and a second backend. Until those exist, this is a proven contract and unproven software.

CHAPTER 09

Open questions, and the assumptions I had to invent

Two questions the spec deliberately leaves open, and — separated out, so nothing is buried in the prose — the glosses and drawings this page makes on your behalf.

The two questions the spec leaves open

1 · IS THE REASON VOCABULARY'S NAMING SETTLED?

The spec states the six members and calls them “this run's proposal and the maintainer's to rename in one word”. They are the wire's conditions — a NIP-01 machine-readable prefix being the relay naming its own reason. The composition surfaces them through its own operations by importing this contract, not by citing the spec document.

2 · SHOULD THE SEAM CARRY A RELAY-OBSERVATION SURFACE?

Per-relay health, last error, last EOSE: the interface is cosmetic until a host asks for it, and the spec's lean is to ship the smaller surface. connect already reports the pool's own state under the key the pool files a relay by; whether more than that becomes contract is genuinely open.

What I invented, and want you to correct if it is wrong

None of the following is in the spec. Each is a decision I made to turn a technical document into something readable, and each is the kind of thing that should be corrected in one line rather than argued about:

  1. “The module” means the spec and this implementation together. Where the two could be confused I say which one I mean. The spec (nostr-io/nostr-io.md) is the authority; the code (nostr-io/ts/) is its first implementation, and it is the only one.
  2. Every plain-language gloss is mine. The ones most likely to be wrong: the seam for the contract boundary; pool for the injected transport; policy for the host's answer to “which relays”; ack for one relay's acknowledgement; ceiling for the stated event bound; deadline for the caller's wall-clock budget; refusal for a typed failure value; fingerprint for a sha256 checksum. The protocol name (NIP-45) is given as a one-line meaning rather than as a specification.
  3. The grouping of the six operations into a three-band list — connect/target, read, write and count — is mine. The spec lists them flat.
  4. The bounds picture and the two-block timeline are my drawings. The spec carries the bounds as statements and the freeze record carries the story as prose; a picture necessarily chooses a layout.
  5. The reading of each of the six reasons is mine where the spec does not spell one out. The vocabulary members and the mapping's sources are the spec's and the evidence's; the plain-language line attached to each button in chapter 4 is this page's.
  6. I chose the balance of this page: the measured wins are given the numbers they earned, and the gaps are given a whole chapter and the last word. If that balance is wrong for you, the balance is what to change — the facts are the artifacts'.
  7. Every number on this page is quoted from the module's evidence captures (01-build-and-tests.txt, 02-invariant-falsification.txt) or from the freeze record (runs/2026-09-30-first-usable-version/freeze-nostr-io.md) rather than recomputed by me. I did not re-run the suite to reproduce the 24/174 totals from scratch.
  8. I assumed you would rather read one honest page than four cautious ones — so where a record says “READ, not executed”, this page says “READ, not executed” rather than “verified”.

Where this page and those disagree, they are right. It is art, not evidence: it adds no statement to the module and must never be quoted as proof of anything.

Self-contained and offline: no web font, no analytics, no network request of any kind. The motion library is vendored beside this file in vendor/, and with JavaScript off, or the library missing, or “reduce motion” set in your operating system, the whole page is simply visible and still — every chapter, every number, every warning.
